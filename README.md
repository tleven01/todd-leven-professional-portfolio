# My Approach to Technology, Customers, and Problem Solving

### Todd Leven | Senior IT Account Executive | Public Sector / SLED | Cloud | SaaS | Cybersecurity | AI

I have spent much of my career helping customers solve complex technology and business problems. My goal has never been simply to present products. I want to understand the environment, ask better questions, bring the right people together, and help customers make better decisions.

Throughout my career in technology sales, I have been fortunate to work alongside talented Solutions Engineers, technical specialists, executives, and customers who understood their environments at a very deep level. I was often impressed by how quickly a strong technical resource could see relationships between systems, identify risks, and translate complexity into something understandable.

Those experiences also made me reflect on my own role.

I never set out to become an engineer, and I do not believe an Account Executive needs to be the most technical person in the room. But I did want to become a stronger resource for the customer. I wanted to understand enough about the customer's environment to ask better questions, recognize patterns, contribute meaningfully to technical conversations, and complement—not simply depend on—the Solutions Engineers and specialists I worked with.

That became the origin of a broader professional goal: **continuously expand the tools, knowledge, and methods I can use to help customers, colleagues, and teams solve problems more effectively.**

Over time, that has led me to deepen my understanding of cloud, cybersecurity, data, analytics, artificial intelligence, and emerging technologies. More recently, it has also led me to formalize my interest in data analysis through the **Google Data Analytics Professional Certificate**, which I am currently working toward completing.

The objective has never been to collect technology buzzwords or become something I am not. It is much simpler:

> **I want to understand problems more clearly, contribute more intelligently to the conversation, and help people make better decisions.**

## Start With the Problem, Not the Product

Technology sales can become overly product-driven very quickly.

A customer says they have a security problem, so the conversation immediately turns to a security product. They mention cloud modernization, so vendors begin discussing cloud platforms. They want better analytics, so the discussion moves directly to software.

Sometimes that is appropriate. But the stated requirement is not always the real problem.

I have found that better conversations often begin with more fundamental questions:

What changed? Why is this important now? What is creating the most friction? What has already been tried? What happens if nothing changes? How will we know whether an improvement actually worked?

Those questions shift the discussion away from what we sell and toward what the customer is truly trying to accomplish.

That is where I believe an effective Account Executive creates real value.

## Using Data to Replace Assumptions With Evidence

One area I have become increasingly interested in is data analysis.

The value of data is not simply producing a chart or dashboard. The real value is using information to challenge assumptions, identify patterns, uncover root causes, and create better questions.

Data can help move a conversation from:

> "We think this is the problem."

to:

> "Here is what the evidence suggests may be happening."

That distinction matters.

Consider a customer with a complicated cybersecurity environment. Over time, many organizations accumulate multiple security products purchased by different teams for different needs. Eventually, they may find themselves with overlapping capabilities, unclear ownership, increasing costs, and uncertainty about whether meaningful security gaps still exist.

The easy sales response would be to introduce another product.

A better approach is to first understand the environment.

By examining technologies currently deployed, licensing and renewal information, utilization, support trends, incident history, overlapping capabilities, operational ownership, and areas of risk, the customer can begin to see where they may be overinvested, where tools are underused, and where genuine gaps remain.

That leads to a more valuable discussion:

**What should remain? What should be consolidated? Where are we overspending? Where does risk remain? What outcome are we trying to improve?**

The analysis does not replace the customer conversation or technical validation. It gives everyone a more objective starting point.

## Better Territory Management Through Analysis

The same analytical thinking applies to sales management.

A large territory may contain dozens or hundreds of accounts, but not every account deserves the same amount of attention at the same time.

A salesperson can use data to evaluate historical revenue, installed technologies, renewal dates, contract usage, customer engagement, budget cycles, infrastructure age, cloud adoption, security investment, support activity, and prior opportunities.

Patterns begin to emerge.

A customer increasing cloud adoption without a corresponding increase in security investment may deserve a broader security conversation.

An organization with aging infrastructure and rising support issues may be approaching a modernization decision.

A large account spending heavily in one technology category but very little in adjacent areas may contain meaningful whitespace.

Data does not replace the relationship or the sales conversation. It helps determine **where the conversation may be most valuable**.

## Improving Pipeline Decisions

Sales pipelines also benefit from analytical thinking.

An opportunity can remain open in a CRM for months and still appear healthy at a glance. But data can reveal a different story.

Opportunity age, time spent in each sales stage, close-date movement, customer engagement, conversion rates, average deal size, win/loss history, and forecast accuracy can help distinguish between opportunities that are genuinely progressing and those that are simply still open.

That creates better questions:

**What evidence do we have that the customer is moving forward? What has changed since the last meeting? Who is missing from the decision process? Is there a defined business problem, budget, and next step?**

Better analysis produces better pipeline discipline and more credible forecasting.

## Using AI to Improve Preparation and Decision-Making

Artificial intelligence adds another useful layer to this process.

I see AI primarily as a **research and thinking tool**.

Used thoughtfully, AI can help an Account Executive research an organization before a meeting, summarize public information, identify likely business priorities, compare technologies, organize account history, prepare discovery questions, identify risks, and develop stronger account plans.

The important point is that AI should improve judgment, not replace it.

A useful AI output should make me better prepared for the customer conversation. It should help me ask a better question—not convince me that I already know the answer.

The customer still provides the context. Technical experts still validate the technology. Human judgment still determines what matters.

## Complementing the Solutions Engineer

My original motivation for exploring many of these tools was my desire to become a better partner to Solutions Engineers.

Earlier in my career, I sometimes felt overly dependent on technical specialists during complex customer conversations. Over time, I realized the solution was not to try to become an engineer.

The solution was to become better at my own role.

A strong Account Executive can add value by understanding the customer's business problem, learning enough about the technology environment to ask intelligent questions, identifying the right technical resources, connecting technical discussions to business priorities, recognizing stakeholder concerns, and helping maintain momentum.

The Solutions Engineer brings technical depth.

The Account Executive can bring **context, relationships, commercial understanding, coordination, and business perspective**.

When those roles complement each other well, the customer gets a much better experience.

## Connecting Technology to Business Outcomes

Technology is most valuable when it improves something meaningful.

A cybersecurity solution should reduce risk.

A cloud platform should improve agility, scalability, reliability, or cost management.

A data platform should improve access to information and decision-making.

A modernization project should improve resilience, productivity, service delivery, or customer experience.

One example from my public-sector experience involved the Colorado Division of Motor Vehicles and a Medallia customer-experience platform using AI-driven sentiment analysis. The engagement improved operational visibility and contributed to an approximately **30% reduction in customer wait times**.

The technology mattered, but the outcome mattered more.

That is the kind of connection I try to maintain in customer conversations:

**What business result are we actually trying to improve?**

## Helping Colleagues Think Through Problems

I apply the same problem-solving approach when working with colleagues.

When someone brings me a stalled opportunity or difficult customer situation, I do not believe the most useful response is always to tell them what to do.

Often, the better approach is to help organize the problem.

What do we actually know? What are we assuming? What has the customer told us directly? Who is missing from the conversation? What business outcome matters? Where is the risk? What information would change our recommendation?

Sometimes the answer is better discovery.

Sometimes it is technical validation.

Sometimes it is an executive conversation.

Sometimes it is procurement.

And sometimes the right answer is recognizing that an opportunity is not ready.

Helping someone reach a sound conclusion is often more useful than simply giving them an answer.

## Mentoring as Collaborative Problem Solving

That philosophy is also why I enjoy mentoring.

I did not have a consistent mentor early in my career, and I know I would have benefited from one.

For me, mentoring is not about positioning myself as the person with all the answers. It is about helping someone think through situations with greater clarity and confidence.

The best mentoring conversations involve listening, understanding, questioning, sharing relevant experience, evaluating options, and deciding on a path forward.

A mentor can provide perspective, lessons, and experience, but the person being mentored should ultimately develop their own judgment.

The goal is not dependence.

The goal is growth.

For me, mentoring is also an opportunity to **give back, make a meaningful difference, and continue learning from others at the same time.**

## Continuous Learning as Part of the Job

Technology changes too quickly to rely only on past experience.

That is why I continue investing time in areas such as data analytics, artificial intelligence, cloud computing, cybersecurity, emerging SaaS technologies, customer experience, and process improvement.

My interest in data analytics is a good example.

I am nearing completion of the **Google Data Analytics Professional Certificate** because I believe stronger analytical skills will make me more effective in sales, consulting, and customer problem solving.

I am not pursuing it because I want to become a full-time data analyst.

I am pursuing it because I want another way to understand problems, evaluate evidence, identify patterns, and contribute to better decisions.

## What I Want Customers and Colleagues to Expect From Me

I want people who work with me to expect curiosity, preparation, collaboration, sound business judgment, and continuous learning.

I want to understand the problem before recommending an answer.

I want to use research, data, AI, customer history, and available resources to make conversations more productive.

I want the right people involved and the best ideas on the table.

I want technology recommendations to connect to outcomes such as risk reduction, efficiency, customer experience, resilience, growth, or return on investment.

And whether I am working with a customer, colleague, manager, or mentee, I want the objective to be the same:

**Leave the person or organization in a better position than where we started.**

## The Principle Behind It

> **Technology creates the most value when we understand the problem clearly, use evidence intelligently, involve the right people, and measure whether the outcome actually improved.**

That is the kind of work I enjoy.

It is also the kind of Account Executive, colleague, consultant, and mentor I continue working to become.
